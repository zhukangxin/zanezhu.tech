## Introduction: the model is not what fails, the management is

Most enterprise AI efforts do not fail because the model is not good enough. They fail because they are managed the wrong way. Treat AI as a one-off IT purchase, start from architecture and deployment, and leave strategy, people and operations unchanged, and the result is almost always the same: busy pilots and no change in the P&L.

My argument is that AI creates enterprise-level value only when five conditions hold together. Miss any one, and the effort fails.

1. **The CEO's sustained strategic commitment.** AI is a strategy the CEO personally leads and funds across years, not a line in the CIO's budget.
2. **AI treated as talent.** Like a new hire, AI has a role, responsibilities, performance measures and clear limits on what it may do.
3. **Ongoing development, not one-time delivery.** Models, data, prompts and knowledge bases need continuous coaching, and so do the employees who work with them.
4. **Business process redesign first.** Ask "how should this work be done?" before asking "where does AI go?"
5. **A changed operating model.** Structure, performance metrics, risk controls, decision rights and the split of work between people and AI all have to change.

The opposite approach is remarkably consistent: treat AI only as technology, start only from the bottom of the stack, and focus on one-off deployment, applications and architecture.

In my [previous article](/articles/manage-ai-like-talent/) I argued that AI should be managed like talent. This article moves up a level, to the CEO and the organization, and asks why AI transformations that lack these five conditions fail. First the overall evidence, then three failures and two successes.

## The evidence: 70% of the value lies outside the technology

Three major studies point the same way: leadership, people and processes decide whether AI succeeds, not the algorithms.

| Study | Key finding | What it means for this argument |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MIT NANDA, *The GenAI Divide* (July 2025) [[1]](#ref-1) | About 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots show no measurable P&L impact, despite roughly $30–40 billion in enterprise spending. The report blames brittle workflows, weak contextual learning and misalignment with day-to-day operations | Failure comes from unchanged processes and no ongoing learning, not from the model |
| BCG's 10-20-70 rule [[2]](#ref-2) | About 10% of AI value comes from algorithms, 20% from technology and data, and 70% from people and processes. Leading companies plan to upskill more than half their employees on AI, versus about 20% for laggards | Managing only the infrastructure covers 30% of the job |
| McKinsey, *The State of AI in 2025* [[3]](#ref-3) | Only about 6% of companies are AI high performers (AI contributes 5% or more of EBIT). Workflow redesign is one of the strongest drivers of business impact, and high performers are three times as likely to strongly agree that senior leaders show ownership of and commitment to AI | Process redesign plus leadership from the top |
| McKinsey survey, March 2025 [[4]](#ref-4) | CEO oversight of AI governance is one of the factors most correlated with bottom-line impact, especially at large companies | The CEO is a variable, not a bystander |

A McKinsey QuantumBlack leader put the difference simply: giving an employee an AI tool makes an existing task faster, while redesigning a workflow around AI can change the economics of the process [[5]](#ref-5).

One caveat: MIT's 95% figure uses a narrow definition (rapid, measurable P&L impact), and its method has been debated. But three independent studies point in the same direction, so the conclusion is robust.

## Case 1: MD Anderson and IBM Watson, a technology project without strategic governance

**What happened.** Starting in June 2012, the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center worked with IBM on an Oncology Expert Advisor (OEA). After four years and $62.1 million, the system had never been used with patients or piloted outside the center, and the project was halted in September 2016 [[6]](#ref-6).

**Why it failed.**

- **It sat outside the institution's strategy and governance.** A University of Texas System audit found that the project bypassed normal IT procurement. Almost $40 million went to IBM and over $21 million to PwC, nearly all without board approval [[7]](#ref-7). It was one department's flagship, not an institution-wide long-term strategy.
- **The goals kept moving.** The audit found an unfocused strategy that did not meet its goals. Engadget's analysis adds that the focus shifted from leukemia to lung cancer and the technology kept changing, even though participating doctors said they were impressed [[8]](#ref-8).
- **It was never built into clinical workflow.** The hospital switched electronic medical record systems, and the OEA was never integrated with the new one [[7]](#ref-7). AI stayed a bolt-on tool rather than part of how care was delivered.

**What it shows.** Technology was not the main problem; the audit explicitly declined to judge the system's science. The problem was the lack of sustained ownership at the top, the treatment of AI as a one-off, outsourced technology build, and no redesign of clinical processes.

## Case 2: Klarna, AI as a cost-cutting tool rather than talent to develop

**What happened.** Swedish fintech Klarna froze hiring in December 2023 and said its AI assistant handled two-thirds of customer service chats in its first month, the work of 700 agents [[9]](#ref-9). By May 2025, CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski told Bloomberg that service quality had slipped and that Klarna was hiring human agents again. In his words, cost had been "a too predominant evaluation factor" [[10]](#ref-10).

**Why it failed.**

- **Performance was measured only by cost.** The AI's "performance" was counted in conversations handled and headcount saved, not in customer outcomes. That is treating AI as technology. Nobody evaluates a new employee by salary savings alone.
- **Full replacement instead of a staged role.** There was no narrow scope for the AI to handle on its own first, followed by wider authority once it proved itself. Complex and emotional conversations went to the AI as well.
- **The split between people and AI came afterwards.** The corrected model, AI for routine questions and people for high-value conversations, should have been designed before launch.

**For balance.** Klarna has not abandoned AI. The CEO says AI is taking on more customer service work, while the new human hires handle higher-level conversations [[11]](#ref-11). That makes the point: the AI was usable; what failed was a cost-driven, replace-everything approach.

**What it shows.** If AI is not treated as talent, with development and staged responsibility, even a highly visible CEO push ends up paid for in customer experience.

## Case 3: Zillow Offers, CEO strategy without a matching operating model

**What happened.** From 2018, US real estate platform Zillow used pricing algorithms to buy, renovate and resell homes. CEO Rich Barton drove the effort personally, aiming to buy 5,000 homes a month by 2024 [[14]](#ref-14). In November 2021 Zillow shut the business down, taking write-downs of up to $569 million and cutting about 25% of its workforce [[12]](#ref-12).

**Why it failed.**

- **A forecasting model was treated as the whole business.** Zillow could not accurately forecast home prices three to six months out, and unit economics swung more widely than expected [[13]](#ref-13).
- **Operations and risk controls did not keep pace with the algorithm.** Inventory rose from 3,142 homes in Q2 to 9,790 in Q3 [[13]](#ref-13). Buying outran the company's capacity to renovate, resell and manage risk.
- **No human checks or continuous correction.** Barton later said the risk of mispricing homes was greater than anticipated, and that the company had in effect become a leveraged housing trader [[14]](#ref-14).

**What it shows.** This case matters because Zillow did not lack CEO commitment. It shows that all five conditions are required. Strategic enthusiasm alone, with AI treated as a pricing engine tuned once and the operating model and risk controls left behind, still fails, and the bigger the bet, the bigger the loss.

## What success looks like

### DBS Bank: a decade of CEO-led, sustained investment

DBS's economic value from AI grew from S$180 million in 2022 [[15]](#ref-15) to S$370 million in 2023, S$750 million in 2024 and about S$1 billion in 2025 [[16]](#ref-16).

- **Led by the CEO, sustained across leadership terms.** Piyush Gupta became CEO in 2009 and personally drove DBS's shift to data and AI [[17]](#ref-17). Tan Su Shan, who had led the day-to-day rollout of the digital strategy across her businesses, succeeded him in March 2025 and is ramping up the AI transformation [[18]](#ref-18).
- **AI measured by economic value, not project count.** DBS uses test and control groups and counts only the difference as AI value. Its chief data and transformation officer, Nimish Panchmatia, says the hard part is not the technology but people, structure and culture [[19]](#ref-19).
- **The operating model was rebuilt.** DBS reached about S$1 billion not by layering AI tools on old processes but by rewiring its operating models [[20]](#ref-20).

### Moderna: AI planned like talent in the organization's design

- **HR and IT merged.** Moderna combined its human resources and technology functions into one department, led by former CHRO Tracey Franklin as Chief People and Digital Technology Officer [[21]](#ref-21).
- **From workforce planning to work planning.** She is redesigning teams around which work is best done by people and which by technology, so roles are being created, eliminated and reimagined [[22]](#ref-22).
- **AI deployed at scale.** Working with OpenAI, Moderna built more than 3,000 custom GPTs embedded in employees' daily work [[21]](#ref-21).

Moderna's approach is the most literal version of "AI as talent": AI and employees are planned, assigned and developed in the same department and the same design of work.

## Conclusion: two mindsets, two outcomes

These five cases show that AI is not a system you buy once. It is a new kind of workforce that needs strategic leadership, ongoing development and a reshaped organization.

| Dimension | "Technology project" mindset (path to failure) | "Strategy and talent" mindset (path to success) | Cases |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who owns it | The CIO or one department, around governance | The CEO, sustained across leadership terms | MD Anderson vs DBS |
| How it is funded | One-off budget, done at delivery | Ongoing, added to as economic value is proven | MD Anderson vs DBS |
| How AI is seen | A tool to cut cost and replace headcount | Talent with a role, metrics and staged responsibility | Klarna vs Moderna |
| Where it starts | Infrastructure, models, deployment | Business processes and the design of work | Zillow vs DBS |
| Operating model | Unchanged, AI bolted on | Structure, metrics and risk controls rebuilt together | Zillow vs Moderna |

**Six actions for CEOs**

1. **Lead it yourself.** Own AI strategy and governance personally, and commit to three to five years of sustained investment.
2. **Allocate effort by 10-20-70.** Spend most of the effort on people and processes, not on algorithms and platforms.
3. **Give AI a job description.** Define its responsibilities, limits, human review points and KPIs. Start small, and widen its authority once it meets the bar.
4. **Build a development loop.** Keep improving data, knowledge bases and feedback, and retrain employees to work with AI.
5. **Start from processes, not architecture.** Pick a few end-to-end core processes and redesign the split of work between people and AI.
6. **Measure economic value, not project count.** Use controlled comparisons to quantify AI's effect on revenue, cost, risk and customer experience.

A final note: "bound to fail" is a statement about probabilities. Isolated tools can raise individual productivity without any process change, but they rarely reach the P&L. There is no shortcut from a successful pilot to enterprise-level value.
